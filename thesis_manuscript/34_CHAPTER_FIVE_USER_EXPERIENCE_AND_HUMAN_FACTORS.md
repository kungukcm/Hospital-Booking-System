# CHAPTER 5 SUPPLEMENT E. USER EXPERIENCE AND HUMAN FACTORS ANALYSIS

## 1. Introduction

This supplement examines the assistant from a human-factors perspective. It considers how people are likely to experience the system, what makes the interaction usable, and what human conditions shape acceptance or resistance.

## 2. Why Human Factors Matter

A technically correct assistant can still fail if users find it confusing, slow, or intimidating. Human factors matter because real-world use depends on perception, ease of interaction, and confidence in the system’s behavior.

## 3. Usability Dimensions

The assistant can be evaluated across at least six usability dimensions. Learnability refers to how quickly new users can start using the system effectively. Efficiency measures how fast experienced users can complete their tasks. Error tolerance describes how well the system recovers when users make mistakes or provide partial information. Memorability indicates whether users can remember how to use the system on subsequent visits. Satisfaction reflects the overall user experience and whether they feel better off using the system. Accessibility ensures that the system works for users with different abilities, language preferences, and prior technology experience.

## 4. Learnability

The conversational interface is designed to be easy to learn because users do not have to navigate menus or forms. They can state their needs in ordinary language. This lowers the barrier for first-time users.

However, learnability depends on the system asking clear follow-up questions and not shifting unpredictably between topics.

## 5. Efficiency

Efficiency is improved when the assistant reduces repeated explanation. A patient should not need to restate name, service, date, and time multiple times. The deterministic context recovery logic directly supports this goal.

## 6. Error Tolerance

The assistant tolerates partial input better than a static form because it can recover context and request only the missing pieces. This is important in live conversation, where users naturally omit information.

Error tolerance also includes graceful failure. Users should receive guidance when the system cannot complete a task rather than a generic failure message.

## 7. Memorability

Because the assistant follows a structured but conversational flow, users can more easily remember how to use it after the first interaction. Common patterns such as “details, type, date, time” become familiar.

Memorability is stronger when the assistant stays consistent in how it asks for the next step.

## 8. Satisfaction

User satisfaction in this context is likely to come from achieving three things. First is reduced waiting and confusion, as users can get appointment information without sitting on hold or making repeated calls. Second is clear confirmation of success, where users receive explicit verification that their booking was completed. Third is confidence that the appointment is actually booked, knowing that the information has been recorded and they will be recognized when they arrive. Language consistency also contributes to satisfaction because users feel recognized and included rather than treated as outsiders in a foreign system.

## 9. Accessibility

Accessibility is improved through multiple design choices. Bilingual support ensures that speakers of both English and Swahili can interact fully. Natural-language interaction lets users express themselves without learning a specialized command language. Short corrective prompts provide guidance without overwhelming the user with information. Clear slot presentation shows options in easy-to-understand format. Minimal reliance on complex forms avoids requiring advanced digital literacy. These design choices together help users with lower digital literacy or different language preferences.

## 10. Cognitive Load

The assistant reduces cognitive load by breaking the booking process into manageable steps. Instead of asking users to process a large form or a complex portal, it asks one focused question at a time.

This supports a more humane interaction pattern, especially for users under time pressure or stress.

## 11. Trust from a User Perspective

From the user's point of view, trust emerges when the assistant behaves predictably and reliably. The system builds trust by understanding what the user is asking for, asking useful follow-up questions when information is incomplete rather than giving up, successfully booking the appointment as promised, confirming clearly in writing what was booked, and remaining consistent in language and tone throughout the interaction rather than shifting unexpectedly.

## 12. Friction Points

Potential friction points include:

1. users who type very short messages;
2. users who code-switch between languages;
3. users who provide incomplete personal details;
4. users who expect the assistant to know too much without context.

The thesis design intentionally anticipates these conditions.

## 13. Human Support and Escalation

Human factors research suggests that people prefer systems that know when to hand over to a human. The assistant’s escalation and fallback logic therefore supports usability because it preserves user agency when the system cannot safely continue.

## 14. Social Acceptability

A hospital assistant is more likely to be accepted if it feels respectful, competent, and helpful. The assistant’s tone, transparency, and bilingual support contribute to social acceptability.

## 15. Implications for Pilot Design

Human-factors testing in a future pilot should measure:

1. perceived ease of use;
2. clarity of booking steps;
3. confidence in confirmation;
4. language comfort;
5. willingness to reuse the assistant.

## 16. Conclusion

The user experience of a healthcare appointment assistant is shaped by more than response speed. It depends on clarity, recovery behavior, accessibility, and trust. The assistant in this thesis is designed with these human factors in mind.
