# CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Introduction

The deployment of artificial intelligence in healthcare settings reflects a broader global movement toward digital transformation in service delivery. Within this movement, conversational AI systems, commonly referred to as chatbots or virtual assistants, have attracted considerable scholarly and practical attention for their potential to improve patient access, reduce administrative burden, and increase the availability of health information. This literature review synthesizes the existing body of knowledge across five major thematic domains: the historical and technical evolution of chatbot architectures, the application of chatbots in healthcare settings, operational constraints and design imperatives in transactional systems, human factors including trust and patient engagement, and governance and ethical frameworks for responsible deployment.

The review is structured to build progressively toward the specific research gaps that this thesis addresses. Beginning with foundational technology literature, the review contextualizes the emergence of large language models (LLMs) and the distinct challenge they introduce when applied to high-stakes transactional workflows. It then examines healthcare-specific literature, identifying where prior work has succeeded and where critical gaps remain. Throughout, particular attention is paid to the intersection of conversational fluency and transactional reliability, multilingual inclusivity in digital health systems, and the governance structures that must accompany responsible AI deployment in low-resource health contexts such as Kenya.

The review draws from peer-reviewed journal articles, conference proceedings, technical reports, and grey literature relevant to health informatics, artificial intelligence, operations research, and digital health policy. Sources have been evaluated for relevance, methodological rigor, and recency, with emphasis placed on work published after 2015 to reflect the rapid pace of change in AI capabilities. Where foundational older works remain essential to understanding the field, they are included with appropriate contextualization of how the field has evolved since their publication.

## 2.2 Historical and Technical Evolution of Chatbot Systems

### 2.2.1 Rule-Based Systems and the Origins of Conversational Computing

The history of computational conversation begins with Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA program, developed at MIT in 1966, which demonstrated that computers could simulate meaningful conversation through pattern matching and scripted responses. Although ELIZA had no genuine understanding of language, users reported feeling emotionally engaged with the system, an observation that Weizenbaum found troubling and that the research community has continued to grapple with ever since. ELIZA established the fundamental interface paradigm for conversational agents: a system that receives natural language input, processes it according to internal rules, and generates natural language output.

The rule-based approach that ELIZA pioneered remained the dominant paradigm for several decades. Systems built in the 1970s through 1990s, including PARRY (a psychiatric interview simulation), ALICE (Artificial Linguistic Internet Computer Entity), and numerous commercial customer service applications, all relied on hand-crafted rules, keyword matching, and decision trees. These systems were entirely deterministic: the same input would always produce the same output. Their behavior was therefore transparent to developers and predictable to users once they understood the system's domain. However, rule-based systems required extensive manual engineering for each new domain, scaled poorly with vocabulary size, and failed when users expressed requests outside the anticipated vocabulary.

Caldarini et al. (2022) provided a comprehensive survey of chatbot development across multiple generations, noting that rule-based systems achieved success in narrow, well-defined domains but repeatedly demonstrated brittleness when deployed in open-domain settings. The survey documented that as user expectations rose during the proliferation of consumer internet services, the limitations of hand-crafted rules became commercially untenable, driving investment in statistical and machine learning approaches.

### 2.2.2 Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches

The emergence of corpus-based natural language processing in the 1990s and early 2000s enabled a shift from hand-crafted rules to data-driven learning. Systems could now learn from large collections of example conversations, developing probabilistic models of what response was appropriate given a particular input. This statistical revolution brought important gains in coverage and flexibility but introduced new challenges around transparency and interpretability. When a rule-based system failed, a developer could inspect the rules to find the error. When a statistical model failed, the failure was embedded in millions of learned parameters that were difficult to inspect or debug.

Hidden Markov Models, Naive Bayes classifiers, and Support Vector Machines became standard tools for intent classification and entity extraction. These components could accurately identify that a user was requesting an appointment (intent) and had specified a service type and date (entities) with reasonable accuracy on in-domain data. However, the systems remained limited in their ability to maintain conversational context across multiple turns, handle reference resolution (understanding what "it" or "that" referred to in previous messages), and generate novel responses rather than selecting from pre-defined templates.

Retrieval-based approaches that matched user queries to a database of pre-written responses achieved commercial success in customer service applications. These systems avoided the risks of generative models by always producing human-curated responses, but they could only respond to inputs similar to those already in the database. This limitation became acute as user populations diversified and as service domains expanded beyond simple FAQ scenarios.

### 2.2.3 The Deep Learning Revolution and Neural Conversational Models

The application of deep learning to natural language processing, beginning around 2013 with word embedding models and accelerating with sequence-to-sequence architectures in 2014, marked a decisive shift in what conversational systems could achieve. Recurrent neural networks and, later, attention mechanisms enabled systems to process variable-length input sequences and generate contextually appropriate responses without relying on pre-defined templates. These systems could, for the first time, produce genuinely novel responses to novel inputs.

The introduction of the Transformer architecture by Vaswani et al. (2017) and the subsequent development of large pre-trained language models (BERT, GPT, and their successors) transformed the field. Pre-training on vast corpora of text allowed models to develop rich representations of language that could be fine-tuned for specific tasks with relatively little task-specific training data. This transfer learning approach reduced the engineering burden of deploying conversational AI and made sophisticated language understanding accessible to a much wider range of applications.

Caldarini et al. (2022) identified this transition as the most significant development in chatbot technology since the shift from rule-based to statistical approaches, noting that LLM-based systems achieved previously impossible levels of linguistic sophistication. However, the same review documented a critical gap that became evident as LLMs were deployed in practice: the gap between conversational quality and task completion quality. A system that could discuss any topic fluently might still fail reliably at structured tasks requiring schema-compliant tool invocations, mandatory field validation, or deterministic state management.

### 2.2.4 Large Language Models and the Emergence of Tool-Using Agents

The most recent phase of chatbot development is characterized by LLMs with the ability to invoke external tools, APIs, and services. Models like GPT-4, Claude, and similar systems can be given descriptions of available tools and instructed to call them when appropriate, extending their capabilities from language generation to action execution. This development is particularly significant for healthcare administrative applications, where the value of a conversational interface depends on its ability to actually execute transactions: booking appointments, retrieving records, and updating schedules.

However, tool use introduces new failure modes that purely conversational systems do not encounter. Understanding the Limitations of AI Chatbots (n.d.) documented several categories of failure in tool-using LLM systems, including parameter hallucination (generating plausible but invalid parameter values), schema non-compliance (producing tool invocations in incorrect formats), and intent-action mismatch (invoking the wrong tool for a given request). These failures are particularly consequential in healthcare settings because they can result in missed appointments, incorrect bookings, or lost patient information, with direct implications for patient access to care.

The research literature on LLM tool use has grown rapidly in response to these challenges. Schick et al. (2023) examined GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 performance on diverse tool-use tasks and found that while larger models outperformed smaller ones, neither achieved the reliability required for deployment in high-stakes contexts without additional safeguards. Critically, errors did not disappear with increasing model scale; they shifted in character and frequency but remained problematic for transactional applications.

## 2.3 Chatbots in Healthcare: Applications, Opportunities, and Constraints

### 2.3.1 Overview of Healthcare Chatbot Applications

The application of chatbot technology to healthcare has expanded rapidly over the past decade, encompassing a diverse range of use cases from patient education and medication reminders to mental health support and administrative workflow automation. Laranjo et al. (2018) conducted one of the most comprehensive systematic reviews of conversational agents in health care, examining 17 randomized controlled trials and finding evidence that chatbots could improve health knowledge, promote healthy behaviors, and support medication adherence. The review concluded that while the evidence base was promising, most studies had methodological limitations including small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and lack of comparison to alternative interventions.

Cavalcante et al. (2015) reviewed chatbot development in the healthcare field more broadly, examining both commercial deployments and research prototypes. Their review identified appointment scheduling as one of the highest-value use cases for healthcare chatbots, noting that scheduling is high-volume, repetitive, and well-suited to automation. The review also identified key challenges in healthcare chatbot development, including regulatory uncertainty, privacy requirements, and the need for clinical validation that does not apply to non-healthcare applications.

Kim et al. (2023) specifically examined the role of AI chatbots in hospital administrative tasks through a scoping review, identifying appointment management, patient triage support, and information provision as the most common administrative applications. Their review found that while chatbots demonstrated effectiveness in controlled research settings, real-world deployment outcomes were more variable and depended significantly on integration quality, user training, and institutional support for the technology.

### 2.3.2 Mental Health and Patient Education Applications

A substantial portion of the healthcare chatbot literature focuses on mental health applications, which offer a distinctive use case: the conversational interaction itself may have therapeutic value, making the quality of conversation more directly tied to health outcomes than in administrative applications. Proposed Use of Chatbots in Mental Health Support (Hussein, n.d.) examined how chatbot interactions can reduce barriers to mental health care access, particularly in contexts where stigma, cost, or service availability limit access to human professionals.

Abd-Alrazaq et al. (2021) conducted a systematic review of conversational chatbots in mental health, examining 32 studies and finding evidence that chatbots could reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety in some populations. The review noted substantial heterogeneity in outcomes and emphasized that chatbot interventions were more likely to succeed when clearly scoped to specific conditions and user populations. AI-Powered Chatbots for Mental Health Support (2025) extended this analysis to newer LLM-based systems, finding that more sophisticated language models could maintain therapeutic rapport more effectively but introduced new concerns around boundary maintenance, appropriate referral, and the risk of users developing emotional dependence on AI systems.

Exploring the Potential of Chatbots in Mental Health (2023) examined specific mechanisms by which chatbot interactions support mental health outcomes, including psychoeducation, cognitive reframing exercises, mood tracking, and crisis identification. The paper concluded that chatbots are most effective when they operate as adjuncts to human care rather than replacements, a finding with direct relevance to healthcare administrative applications where human oversight remains essential.

### 2.3.3 Virtual Health Assistants and Medication Adherence

The evolving role of virtual health assistants in patient care has been documented across multiple studies examining medication adherence, chronic disease management, and patient engagement. Bickmore et al. (2019) examined patient and clinician perceptions of a virtual health assistant designed to support medication adherence, finding that patients with chronic conditions responded positively to consistent, personalized reminders and were more likely to maintain medication schedules when supported by the conversational assistant. Clinicians expressed concern about liability and about the potential for patients to overly rely on the assistant rather than maintaining direct communication with care providers.

Bibault et al. (2019) evaluated a chatbot designed to address patients' questions in oncology, noting that the high-anxiety nature of cancer care created particular demands for accuracy and emotional sensitivity in system responses. The study found that patients valued the availability of the system outside normal clinic hours and appreciated the ability to ask questions without feeling they were "bothering" clinical staff. However, the study also found that patients expected the system to have access to their personal clinical records, an expectation that highlighted the need for integration with clinical information systems.

The Evolving Role of Virtual Health Assistants (n.d.) synthesized evidence across multiple clinical domains, concluding that virtual health assistants achieve the most consistent value in scenarios where the service is clearly scoped, the expected interaction patterns are relatively predictable, and the chatbot is positioned as a complement to rather than replacement for human clinical judgment. This finding directly supports the scope decisions in the present thesis, which positions the appointment assistant as an administrative tool that supplements rather than replaces scheduling staff.

### 2.3.4 Technical Chatbots and Telemedicine Integration

The integration of chatbots with telemedicine platforms represents a growing area of application with particular relevance to healthcare access in low-resource settings. The Health ChatBots in Telemedicine (n.d.) examined how conversational AI can serve as the initial interface for remote healthcare consultations, performing triage, collecting patient history information, and scheduling consultations. This application is particularly valuable in contexts where telehealth capacity is limited and initial screening can route patients to appropriate care levels.

The review noted that telemedicine chatbots face distinctive challenges not present in in-person settings: the inability to observe non-verbal communication, the risk of misinterpretation of text descriptions of symptoms, and the need to maintain patient engagement across asynchronous communication. For administrative applications such as appointment booking, however, the telemedicine context removes some of the barriers present in clinical applications: users are already comfortable with digital communication, the information required is structured and well-defined, and the stakes of individual interactions are primarily administrative rather than clinical.

## 2.4 Technical Dimensions: Reliability, Schema Compliance, and Tool-Use in LLM Systems

### 2.4.1 The Reliability Problem in LLM-Based Transactional Systems

The deployment of LLM-based conversational systems in transactional settings has revealed a systematic challenge that does not appear in purely information-provision applications: the gap between linguistic fluency and operational reliability. A system that generates contextually appropriate, grammatically correct, and factually plausible text may still fail to execute structured operations correctly. This gap has been documented across multiple domains including software code generation, database query formulation, and API tool invocation.

Technical Metrics Used to Evaluate Healthcare Chatbots (n.d.) provided a systematic examination of evaluation approaches for healthcare conversational systems, distinguishing between language quality metrics (coherence, fluency, relevance), task completion metrics (booking completion rate, error rate, recovery rate), and safety metrics (scope adherence, appropriate escalation, avoidance of clinical overreach). The paper argued that most existing evaluation focuses on language quality metrics because they are easier to measure automatically, while task completion and safety metrics are more difficult to evaluate but are ultimately more important for real-world healthcare deployment.

The specific failure modes of LLM systems in transactional contexts have been systematically documented. These include parameter omission, where the model generates a tool call with missing required fields; type errors, where parameters are generated in the wrong format; hallucination of tool names, where the model invents tools that do not exist; and schema drift, where the model generates structurally valid-appearing but semantically incorrect parameter values. Each of these failure modes can result in backend system errors that the user experiences as unexplained failures.

### 2.4.2 Deterministic Controls as Reliability Architecture

In response to the reliability challenges of pure LLM systems, the research literature has converged on a hybrid architecture recommendation: combining LLM flexibility for language understanding with deterministic controls for transaction-critical operations. This approach draws on established software engineering principles of separation of concerns, defensive programming, and the distinction between what a system understands and what it does.

A Literature Survey of Recent Advances in Chatbots (Caldarini et al., 2022) included discussion of this hybrid approach, noting that the most reliable commercial chatbot deployments combine a neural language understanding layer with a deterministic action execution layer. The survey cited examples from banking, insurance, and telecommunications where this architecture had achieved high reliability in production settings.

The theoretical foundation for this approach can be traced to the concept of a human-in-the-loop system design, where consequential actions are validated at multiple points before execution. In the context of conversational AI, this means that a booking transaction is not executed based solely on what the LLM infers from conversation; it requires explicit validation that all mandatory parameters are present and valid before the backend system is engaged. This validation can be implemented deterministically, using rule-based checks that cannot be misled by conversational context.

EREBOTS: Privacy-Compliant Agent-Based Platform (2021) demonstrated a practical implementation of this principle in a healthcare context, describing an agent-based architecture where multiple specialized modules handled different aspects of health information processing. The privacy-compliant design of EREBOTS required that certain operations could only proceed when specific data conditions were satisfied, regardless of the conversational context, providing a direct precedent for the guardrail architecture in this thesis.

### 2.4.3 State Machine Approaches to Workflow Management

State machines, mathematical models where a system can be in exactly one of a finite number of states at any given time, have been widely applied to chatbot workflow management as a mechanism for ensuring logical progression through multi-step interactions. In the context of appointment booking, a state machine defines the valid states of the booking workflow (e.g., collecting patient details, confirming service type, selecting date, selecting time, confirming booking) and the conditions under which transitions between states are permitted.

This approach ensures that the system cannot attempt to execute a booking without first passing through all required information-collection states. It makes the workflow explicit and auditable, allowing developers to verify that all paths through the workflow are valid and that no paths allow skipping required information collection. For healthcare applications where data completeness is a regulatory and operational requirement, state machine workflow management provides an architectural guarantee of completeness that LLM-only approaches cannot offer.

Natural Language Chatbots in Biomedical Contexts (2023) included analysis of workflow management approaches in healthcare chatbots, comparing tree-based, flow-based, and agent-based architectures. The paper found that for structured administrative tasks with well-defined completion criteria, state machine approaches consistently outperformed more flexible but less predictable agent-based approaches in terms of task completion rate and operational error frequency.

### 2.4.4 LLM-Based Language Learning and Natural Language Understanding

LLM-Based Chatbots in Language Learning (2024) provided relevant insights into how LLM-based systems handle linguistic variation, including domain-specific vocabulary, colloquial expressions, and cross-language understanding. Although the paper focused on educational applications rather than healthcare, its findings about LLM capabilities in understanding non-standard linguistic inputs are directly relevant to the present thesis's context of Kenyan healthcare users who may combine English and Swahili expressions or use colloquial terms for medical services.

The paper found that LLM-based systems significantly outperformed earlier statistical systems in understanding varied expressions of the same semantic content, including non-standard vocabulary, dialectal variation, and code-switching between languages. This capability is particularly valuable in contexts like Kenya where users may naturally move between languages within a single conversation.

The paper also noted a critical caveat: while LLMs excel at understanding natural language variation, their ability to generate language-consistent outputs across entire conversations is less robust. In interactive systems, the model's output language can drift based on the immediate conversational context, potentially producing outputs in an unintended language even when the user has been consistently communicating in another. This finding directly motivates the approach in this thesis of using deterministic localization for transaction-critical outputs rather than relying on LLM-generated translations.

## 2.5 Queue Management and Operational Optimization in Healthcare

### 2.5.1 Patient Flow and Queue Theory in Healthcare Settings

The management of patient queues and appointment scheduling has been studied extensively within operations research, health services research, and industrial engineering. The foundational insight from queue theory, that systems with random arrival patterns and variable service times will inevitably produce waiting, has been applied to healthcare settings to understand and reduce delays. Healthcare queues have characteristics that distinguish them from commercial queuing situations, including the variability of service duration (a routine appointment and a complex consultation may take vastly different amounts of time), the urgency gradient (some patients require immediate attention while others can wait longer), and the no-show phenomenon (patients who book appointments but do not attend).

The impact of appointment scheduling on queue performance has been studied extensively. Research consistently shows that clustered demand, where many patients book appointments at the same times because of preference patterns or structural availability, produces predictable peak congestion that reduces service quality and patient experience. When information about expected congestion is made available to patients at booking time, demand naturally redistributes toward less-busy periods.

Technical Metrics Used to Evaluate Healthcare Chatbots (n.d.) specifically addressed chatbot contributions to queue management, noting that conversational booking interfaces are uniquely positioned to present patients with real-time or predicted congestion information and to guide them toward less-busy appointment times. The paper argued that this information provision function could improve queue management outcomes without requiring changes to clinic operations or scheduling policies, making it a high-value, low-implementation-complexity enhancement.

### 2.5.2 Predictive Analytics and Demand Forecasting in Healthcare

The application of predictive analytics to healthcare demand forecasting has a well-established literature, with models ranging from simple historical averaging to complex machine learning approaches. Healthcare demand exhibits strong temporal patterns: certain days of the week, certain times of day, and certain periods in the year consistently produce higher or lower patient volumes across most service types. These patterns, combined with service-specific characteristics such as typical appointment duration and staff availability, provide a basis for predicting expected congestion at different time slots.

The value of such predictions for patient decision support depends on calibration quality. A prediction that consistently overestimates waiting times will cause patients to avoid times that are actually available, while a prediction that underestimates waiting times will fail to guide patients toward genuinely less-busy slots. The literature on decision support under uncertainty suggests that presenting predictions with appropriate confidence indication (e.g., "typically low wait" rather than "exactly 15 minutes") maintains decision support value while avoiding the trust-damaging consequences of overconfident predictions that do not materialize.

Ensuring Consumer Satisfaction with Chatbots (2022) noted that chatbot interactions around service wait times and availability were particularly sensitive: users who received accurate predictions reported higher satisfaction with the system overall, while users who received inaccurate predictions expressed reduced trust in all system outputs, not just the prediction function. This finding supports the design decision in this thesis to present queue recommendations in interpretable, relative terms rather than with false precision.

### 2.5.3 Patient-Facing Queue Information and Behavior Change

The provision of queue information to patients as a mechanism for influencing appointment timing behavior has been studied in both healthcare and service industry contexts. The theoretical basis for this intervention is information asymmetry: when patients do not know which times are less busy, they default to their preferred times regardless of system load. When given accurate information about expected congestion, they are more likely to shift toward alternatives when the quality difference is made salient.

Factors Influencing Patient Engagement in Mental Health Chatbots (n.d.) examined the characteristics of chatbot interactions that promoted patient engagement and behavior change, finding that clear, actionable information presented in a supportive rather than directive manner produced the highest engagement. The paper's findings about framing effects, specifically that presenting alternatives as "a lower-wait option is available at this time" is more effective than presenting raw statistical data, informed the design of the queue recommendation interface in this thesis.

The integration of queue prediction with conversational booking systems has not been widely studied in the literature, representing a gap that this thesis addresses. Most existing work either examines queue management as a clinic operations problem or examines chatbot booking as a patient communication problem, without integrating the two perspectives. The present work demonstrates that these perspectives are complementary and that conversational booking interfaces are natural vehicles for delivering queue information in a patient-friendly format.

## 2.6 Multilingual AI and Digital Health Equity

### 2.6.1 Language Diversity as a Digital Health Determinant

Digital health equity scholarship has established that technology interventions do not produce equitable outcomes across all populations automatically. Factors including literacy, digital literacy, device access, connectivity, language, and cultural factors all mediate the relationship between technology availability and health benefit. Among these factors, language is particularly fundamental: a health service that operates in a language the patient does not use comfortably is effectively inaccessible to that patient, regardless of its technical capabilities.

In Kenya, the official languages are English and Swahili (Kiswahili), with English serving as the primary language of government administration, formal education, and healthcare documentation while Swahili is the national language used in everyday communication, broadcasting, and informal contexts. The majority of Kenyan citizens are more comfortable with Swahili in informal contexts, particularly for services with emotional significance such as healthcare. Digital health services that operate only in English systematically disadvantage Swahili-preferring users, a group that disproportionately includes users from lower-income backgrounds, older age groups, and rural areas.

Chatbots as a New User Interface for Providing Health Information to Young People (2018) highlighted the importance of language accessibility in digital health communications, noting that young people in multilingual settings were significantly more likely to engage with health information services in their preferred language. The paper also documented a pattern that has broader relevance: even when users could communicate in a non-preferred language, the cognitive load of doing so reduced engagement quality and comprehension accuracy, with direct implications for healthcare settings where accurate communication is critical.

### 2.6.2 Technical Challenges in Multilingual Chatbot Systems

Implementing genuine multilingual support in a conversational AI system requires more than simply translating the user interface. Deep multilingual support requires language detection that can identify the user's preferred language from conversational input, including cases where users code-switch between languages; language-consistent response generation that maintains the same language throughout a conversation unless the user switches; and deterministic localization of transaction-critical outputs that cannot be left to probabilistic language model generation.

Understanding How Chatbots Work: An Exploratory Study (2021) examined the technical mechanisms underlying chatbot language processing, including the role of intent classification, entity extraction, and response generation in determining chatbot linguistic behavior. The paper noted that most commercial chatbot frameworks provide basic language detection but that maintaining language consistency across an entire multi-turn conversation remains a challenge, particularly in systems where the conversational context may contain mixed-language input that confuses language detection algorithms.

The technical approach in this thesis of using deterministic localization for transaction-critical outputs addresses this challenge by removing language-consistency decisions from the probabilistic language model entirely for the most critical messages. While the LLM handles conversational understanding and generation (where some linguistic flexibility is acceptable), booking confirmations, appointment type labels, and error messages are generated through deterministic lookup tables that guarantee language consistency regardless of conversational context.

### 2.6.3 Code-Switching and Mixed-Language Communication

A distinctive feature of multilingual contexts like Kenya is code-switching: the practice of alternating between languages within a single conversation or even within a single sentence. Kenyan users communicating in digital contexts frequently mix English and Swahili naturally, and a healthcare chatbot that requires strict single-language input would create friction for these users.

LLM-Based Chatbots in Language Learning (2024) documented LLM capabilities in handling code-switched input, finding that large language models pre-trained on multilingual corpora could interpret mixed-language inputs with reasonable accuracy. This capability is significant for the Kenyan context: rather than requiring users to commit to a single language, the system can accept mixed-language input and respond in the user's demonstrated preferred language (typically indicated by the dominant language in their messages).

The practical implication for system design is that language detection should be treated as a preference signal that guides output language selection rather than a strict classifier that gates input acceptance. A user who writes primarily in Swahili but includes English medical terminology should receive responses in Swahili. A user who writes primarily in English but uses some Swahili phrases should receive responses in English. This nuanced language handling requires more sophisticated language context management than simple language classification.

### 2.6.4 Digital Health Equity in Sub-Saharan African Contexts

The specific context of AI deployment in Sub-Saharan African healthcare systems introduces considerations that are less prominent in literature generated from high-income country settings. Connectivity infrastructure in Kenya and similar contexts is predominantly mobile rather than fixed broadband, with significant variation in connection quality between urban and rural areas. Mobile-first design is therefore not merely a convenience but a practical requirement for reaching the intended user population.

Ethical Considerations in Using Artificial Intelligence Chatbots for Culturally Sensitive Mental Health Support in African Psychotherapy (n.d.) addressed the cultural dimensions of AI deployment in African health contexts, arguing that systems designed primarily for Western cultural assumptions may fail to serve African populations effectively. The paper identified specific cultural factors including different norms around communication directness, different approaches to discussing health concerns, and different expectations about the relationship between patient and care provider. While this paper focused on mental health rather than administrative services, its methodological insights about cultural adaptation of AI systems apply broadly.

For administrative chatbots in the Kenyan context, cultural considerations include the appropriate level of formality in system communication, the role of respect and deference in healthcare interactions, and the expectation of hospitality and relationship building that characterizes customer service in many Kenyan contexts. The system's conversational style should reflect these cultural norms rather than simply translating Western customer service templates into Swahili.

Revolutionizing E-Health: The Transformative Role of AI-Powered Chatbots (n.d.) examined AI-driven health communication systems in emerging economy contexts, emphasizing that the transformative potential of chatbots in these settings is particularly high precisely because existing service delivery is often most constrained. When patients face significant barriers to accessing scheduling and information services through traditional channels (limited phone access, inability to visit facilities in person, limited opening hours), chatbot availability across mobile devices can dramatically expand access.

## 2.7 Patient Engagement, Trust, and Adoption of Conversational Healthcare Systems

### 2.7.1 The Technology Acceptance Framework in Healthcare AI

Understanding why patients do or do not adopt and use health technology has been studied extensively using the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) and its healthcare-specific extensions. The TAM framework identifies perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as the primary determinants of technology acceptance, with additional factors including social influence, facilitating conditions, and trust playing important roles in healthcare contexts.

In the chatbot-specific literature, trust has emerged as a particularly critical factor distinguishing healthcare AI adoption from adoption in other domains. Unlike a chatbot that helps choose a restaurant, a healthcare chatbot's failures can directly affect health outcomes, making trust calibration especially important. The Role of Chatbots in Enhancing Customer Service (2024) examined trust dynamics in service chatbots across multiple industries, finding that healthcare emerged as the domain where trust was most carefully calibrated and where single failures had the most negative impact on subsequent usage intentions.

The challenge of trust in AI systems is compounded by the often opaque nature of AI decision-making. When a chatbot responds unexpectedly, users often cannot understand why the system behaved as it did. This opacity undermines trust in ways that human service failures do not, because human errors can be attributed to understandable causes (distraction, misunderstanding, lack of information) while AI errors may seem random or incomprehensible.

### 2.7.2 Factors Influencing Patient Engagement with Health Chatbots

Factors Influencing Patient Engagement in Mental Health Chatbots (n.d.) identified several predictors of sustained patient engagement with conversational health systems. Clarity of purpose emerged as the most important factor: patients were more likely to continue using systems that clearly communicated what they could and could not do. Systems that attempted to serve too many functions or that had unclear boundaries of competence generated uncertainty that discouraged repeated use.

Consistency of behavior was identified as the second most important factor. Users developed mental models of how the chatbot would respond to different types of input, and violations of these models, even when the violation was technically correct behavior, disrupted engagement. This finding has important implications for the design of deterministic controls: the predictability that guardrails introduce is not merely a technical property but a user experience feature that supports engagement.

Responsiveness to user emotional state was identified as a third important factor, particularly in health contexts where anxiety, confusion, and frustration are common. Users were more likely to persist through difficult interactions when the chatbot acknowledged their emotional state and adapted its communication approach accordingly. This finding underscores the importance of empathic response generation in healthcare chatbot design.

Empathic Response Generation in Chatbots (Spring, n.d.) examined the technical mechanisms for generating empathic responses in conversational AI systems, discussing approaches ranging from sentiment detection and response selection to the use of LLMs for contextually appropriate emotional acknowledgment. The paper argued that empathic responses in administrative contexts do not require clinical-level emotional intelligence but do require acknowledgment of the user's emotional context, particularly when errors occur and the user expresses frustration. This guidance informed the error recovery messaging approach in the present thesis.

### 2.7.3 Trust Repair After System Failures

Service recovery literature, originally developed in the context of human service failures, has been applied to digital service contexts with important findings about how systems can rebuild trust after failures. The service recovery paradox, where well-handled failures can produce higher trust than no failure at all, has been demonstrated in some digital contexts, suggesting that how a system handles errors matters as much as the frequency of errors.

For healthcare chatbots, trust repair after failures requires honest acknowledgment of what went wrong, clear guidance on next steps, and rapid restoration of service. Ensuring Consumer Satisfaction with Chatbots (2022) examined consumer satisfaction in chatbot interactions across service industries, finding that service recovery quality was one of the strongest predictors of overall satisfaction. Chatbots that responded to failure with clear, helpful error messages and recovery paths produced significantly higher satisfaction ratings than those that produced generic error notifications or fell silent.

Use of Chatbots for Customer Service in MSMEs (n.d.) similarly documented the importance of clear error communication in chatbot interactions, noting that users were generally willing to tolerate a reasonable failure rate if the failures were clearly communicated and recoverable. Users were far less tolerant of silent failures, where the chatbot appeared to succeed but did not actually complete the requested task. This finding is directly relevant to healthcare administrative chatbots: a user who receives a booking confirmation only to discover the appointment was not actually created would experience a trust failure far more damaging than an explicit booking failure with recovery guidance.

### 2.7.4 Brand Representation and Institutional Identity in Healthcare Chatbots

Chatbots for Brand Representation in Comparison with Traditional Websites (2016), while predating the current LLM era, established foundational principles about how conversational interfaces represent institutional identity that remain relevant. The paper noted that chatbot interactions shape users' perceptions of the institution as much as the physical service environment does. A chatbot that communicates clearly, professionally, and helpfully projects an institutional image of competence and care; a chatbot that produces confusing, inconsistent, or error-prone interactions projects the opposite.

For healthcare institutions, this brand representation function is particularly significant because institutional trust is central to healthcare seeking behavior. Patients who trust their hospital are more likely to follow clinical recommendations, adhere to treatment plans, and recommend the institution to others. A chatbot that undermines institutional trust through poor performance, even in the administrative domain, can have wider implications for the institution's relationship with its patient population.

Chatbots and Government Communications in COVID-19 (2020) documented how government health agencies used chatbots during the COVID-19 pandemic to manage unprecedented demand for health information, finding that chatbot communication quality was associated with perceptions of institutional competence and crisis management effectiveness. Institutions whose chatbots provided accurate, timely, and clear information were rated more positively than those with less effective chatbot communications, even controlling for underlying policy quality.

## 2.8 Governance and Ethics of Healthcare AI

### 2.8.1 Bioethical Foundations for Healthcare AI Governance

The ethics of healthcare AI is grounded in the same bioethical principles that govern all healthcare activities: beneficence (promoting patient benefit), nonmaleficence (avoiding harm), justice (fair and equitable treatment), and autonomy (respecting patient agency and decision-making). The application of these principles to AI systems requires translation from their original context of individual clinical decisions to the context of algorithmic systems serving large patient populations.

Ethical Considerations in Using Artificial Intelligence Chatbots for Culturally Sensitive Mental Health Support in African Psychotherapy (n.d.) examined the application of bioethical principles to AI systems in African healthcare contexts, arguing that standard frameworks developed in Western academic contexts do not fully address the ethical dimensions of AI deployment in contexts with different cultural, economic, and infrastructural characteristics. The paper proposed extending standard bioethical analysis to include considerations of community benefit, resource justice, and cultural appropriate.

These extended ethical considerations are directly relevant to the present thesis. Beneficence in the Kenyan hospital context requires not just that the system avoids individual harm but that it actively improves access for populations who have historically been underserved by digital health services. Justice requires attention to whether Swahili-speaking users, elderly users, and users with lower digital literacy receive equivalent service quality to users from more advantaged positions. Autonomy requires that the system provides clear information enabling patients to make informed decisions about their healthcare appointments rather than manipulating choices toward administratively convenient outcomes.

### 2.8.2 Data Protection and Privacy in Healthcare AI

Healthcare data is among the most sensitive personal information, with legal protection frameworks in most jurisdictions and strong ethical norms against unauthorized disclosure. For administrative chatbot systems that collect patient identifying information (name, identification number, contact details) and healthcare service preferences, privacy protection is both an ethical obligation and a regulatory requirement.

The data minimization principle, articulated in the European General Data Protection Regulation and reflected in Kenya's Data Protection Act (2019), requires that systems collect only personal data necessary for the stated purpose. For appointment booking, the necessary data elements are limited: patient identification, contact information, service type, and preferred date and time. Collecting medical history, diagnosis information, or other clinical data beyond what is required for scheduling is ethically and legally problematic and expands the harm potential if data breaches occur.

EREBOTS: Privacy-Compliant Agent-Based Platform (2021) demonstrated a technical architecture for privacy-compliant healthcare AI, describing design patterns including data minimization, access control, purpose limitation, and audit logging that collectively enable privacy-by-design implementation. The architecture described in EREBOTS provided direct inspiration for the governance-oriented design in this thesis, particularly the emphasis on collecting only necessary data and maintaining comprehensive audit logs.

### 2.8.3 Accountability, Transparency, and Explainability

Healthcare AI governance requires clear accountability frameworks: when a system makes a consequential error, who is responsible? The institutional deployment of AI introduces complex accountability chains involving system developers, deploying institutions, clinical staff, and possibly patients themselves. Without clear accountability frameworks, system failures can result in unresolved harm and institutional uncertainty about how to respond.

Transparency about how AI systems make decisions is both an ethical requirement and a practical necessity for appropriate use. Clinicians and administrators who do not understand how a chatbot produces its outputs cannot effectively oversee those outputs or recognize when they are incorrect. The explainability requirement for healthcare AI goes beyond technical interpretability of model internals to include operational transparency: clearly communicating to users what the system does, what it does not do, and how it responds to different types of input.

Bibliometric Analysis of Chatbots in Health (Pears and Konstantinidis, 2016) examined the evolution of healthcare chatbot research over time, documenting a progressive shift from purely technical research toward research incorporating ethical, policy, and governance dimensions. The bibliometric analysis showed that governance-related research in healthcare AI was still a relatively underdeveloped area at the time of publication, with rapid growth after 2018 as major AI failures in high-stakes domains prompted policy attention. This observation supports the present thesis's positioning of governance as a core research contribution rather than a secondary consideration.

### 2.8.4 Human Oversight and Escalation in AI-Assisted Healthcare

A principle emerging consistently across healthcare AI governance literature is that AI systems in high-stakes healthcare contexts should maintain clear pathways for human oversight and escalation. Rather than positioning AI as a replacement for human judgment, responsible deployment frames AI as a tool that augments human capacity while maintaining human accountability for consequential decisions.

For administrative chatbot systems, human oversight is implemented through several mechanisms. Escalation pathways ensure that when the chatbot encounters requests it cannot handle or situations that appear to require human judgment, it routes the user to appropriate human staff rather than attempting to resolve the situation through autonomous means. Audit logs provide the data that human supervisors need to review system performance, identify error patterns, and detect cases where the system's behavior may have been inappropriate.

The Evolving Role of Virtual Health Assistants (n.d.) emphasized that the most successful healthcare AI deployments maintain this human-in-the-loop principle, not as a temporary expedient until AI improves but as a permanent feature of responsible deployment. The paper argued that even highly capable AI systems should maintain escalation pathways to human staff because the range of situations encountered in real healthcare settings exceeds what can be anticipated in system design, and human judgment remains essential for novel situations.

### 2.8.5 Healthcare AI Policy and Regulatory Frameworks

The regulatory environment for healthcare AI is developing rapidly across multiple jurisdictions. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration has developed frameworks for Software as a Medical Device that distinguish between administrative software and software performing medical functions, with the latter subject to regulatory oversight. In the European Union, the AI Act introduces risk-based classification for AI systems, with healthcare applications subject to high-risk requirements including human oversight, transparency documentation, and post-market monitoring.

In Kenya, the regulatory context for healthcare AI is defined primarily by the Kenya Data Protection Act (2019), the Kenya Information and Communications Act, and sector-specific regulations from the Ministry of Health. The Data Protection Act establishes requirements for data collection, processing, and storage that apply to all digital systems collecting personal data, including healthcare administrative chatbots. The Act requires explicit consent from data subjects, purpose limitation for collected data, and adequate security measures to protect personal data.

Chatbots and Government Communications in COVID-19 (2020) examined how health regulatory agencies in several countries adapted their oversight approaches during the pandemic to accommodate rapid deployment of chatbot systems for health information dissemination, finding that agencies that had developed clear frameworks for health chatbot governance were able to enable rapid deployment while maintaining safety oversight. This finding supports the present thesis's argument that governance-oriented design should be built into systems from the outset, enabling faster institutional adoption by demonstrating regulatory alignment.

## 2.9 Chatbots in Non-Clinical Service Settings: Transferable Lessons

### 2.9.1 Customer Service Chatbots: Performance and Design Patterns

The customer service chatbot literature offers a well-developed body of evidence on what works and what does not in high-volume, transactional conversational interfaces. Chatbots in Airport Customer Service: Exploring Use Cases and Implications (Auer et al., 2024) examined chatbot deployment in airport environments, a context that shares several characteristics with hospital administrative settings: high volume, time-sensitive transactions, diverse user populations with varying language backgrounds, and significant consequences when systems fail.

The airport context study found that chatbots were most effective for routine, high-frequency queries (flight status, gate information, check-in procedures) but less effective for non-standard situations requiring contextual judgment. The design implication was that chatbots should be designed to handle common cases excellently while maintaining clear escalation pathways for edge cases, rather than attempting to handle all situations through the conversational interface alone. This finding directly parallels the approach in the present thesis: the appointment booking chatbot is designed to handle standard booking, cancellation, and information retrieval tasks with high reliability, while escalating unusual situations to human staff.

### 2.9.2 Chatbots in Educational Contexts

LLM-Based Chatbots in Language Learning (2024) documented the use of chatbot systems for language instruction, an application that requires sophisticated language understanding and generation capabilities. The paper found that LLM-based systems significantly advanced the state of the art in language education chatbots by enabling more natural and varied interactions than earlier rule-based or statistical systems. The findings about LLM language processing capabilities are directly relevant to healthcare chatbot design, confirming that LLMs can handle the linguistic diversity expected in a multilingual Kenyan user population.

The paper also identified design principles for educational chatbots that transfer to healthcare contexts: maintaining a supportive and non-judgmental tone, providing clear guidance when users are confused, adapting explanation depth to apparent user knowledge level, and scaffolding complex tasks by breaking them into manageable steps. These principles align with the present thesis's emphasis on accessible, user-centered design in the healthcare administrative chatbot.

### 2.9.3 Cross-Sector Evidence on Chatbot Effectiveness

Chatbots for Brand Representation in Comparison with Traditional Websites (2016) provided early empirical comparison between chatbot-mediated and web-form-mediated service interactions, finding that users reported higher engagement and satisfaction with conversational interfaces for simple, focused tasks. For complex tasks requiring navigation of extensive information or multiple form submissions, traditional web interfaces performed better. This finding has direct implications for the design scope of the present thesis: focusing the chatbot on a specific, well-defined set of administrative tasks (booking, cancellation, information retrieval) rather than attempting to make it a comprehensive hospital information portal.

The Role of Chatbots in Enhancing Customer Service (2024) documented the business case for chatbot deployment in customer service contexts, finding that well-designed chatbots reduced average handling time, increased first-contact resolution rates, and improved customer satisfaction scores compared to phone-only service channels. The paper identified design quality as the primary determinant of chatbot performance, noting that poorly designed chatbots produced worse outcomes than no chatbot at all, while well-designed chatbots consistently outperformed traditional channels for covered task types.

## 2.10 Design Science Research as a Methodology for Healthcare AI

### 2.10.1 Design Science Research: Foundations and Principles

Design science research (DSR) is a research paradigm focused on the creation and evaluation of purposeful IT artifacts that address specific organizational problems. Unlike behavioral science research that seeks to explain existing phenomena, DSR seeks to create innovations that change the world. The seminal work by Hevner et al. (2004) established design science as a legitimate and rigorous research approach in information systems, articulating seven guidelines for DSR that emphasize the importance of creating useful artifacts, rigorously evaluating them, contributing generalizable knowledge, and communicating findings to appropriate audiences.

The DSR framework developed by Peffers et al. (2007) provided a practical process model for DSR that has been widely adopted in information systems research: identify and motivate a problem, define solution objectives, design and develop the artifact, demonstrate the artifact in relevant scenarios, evaluate the artifact against defined criteria, and communicate findings and contributions. This process model provides a structured approach to DSR that balances rigorous evaluation with practical orientation.

Healthcare is an important application domain for DSR because it combines pressing real-world problems with complex socio-technical systems that cannot be fully understood through purely behavioral or positivist research. The creation of working artifacts, as in the present thesis, generates knowledge about what is possible in healthcare AI design that cannot be obtained through literature review or survey-based research alone.

### 2.10.2 DSR Applied to Healthcare Information Systems

A Literature Survey of Recent Advances in Chatbots (Caldarini et al., 2022) reviewed several design science studies of healthcare chatbot systems, noting that DSR had produced valuable practical knowledge about what architectures, interaction designs, and implementation approaches were effective in healthcare contexts. The review highlighted the advantage of DSR's artifact focus: rather than theorizing about how chatbots could help healthcare, DSR studies demonstrated concretely what they could do and under what conditions.

The present thesis adopts a DSR approach because the central research question, how to design a multilingual AI assistant for hospital appointment support that delivers reliable booking completion, queue-aware recommendations, and policy-aligned governance, is fundamentally a design question rather than an explanatory one. The answer is not found in existing phenomena but must be created through purposeful artifact development. DSR provides the methodological framework for doing this rigorously.

### 2.10.3 Evaluation Standards for Healthcare IT Artifacts

Technical Metrics Used to Evaluate Healthcare Chatbots (n.d.) provided a comprehensive framework for evaluating healthcare conversational AI systems, distinguishing between process metrics (how the system performs during interactions), outcome metrics (what results the system produces), and safety metrics (whether the system operates within appropriate bounds). The paper argued that all three categories of metrics are necessary for a complete evaluation of healthcare chatbot artifacts, and that reliance on process metrics alone (which are the easiest to measure) consistently underestimates the importance of outcome and safety performance.

This evaluation framework directly informed the evaluation approach in the present thesis, which emphasizes booking completion outcomes and error prevention (outcome metrics) alongside conversational quality (process metrics) and scope adherence (safety metrics). The comprehensive evaluation perspective ensures that the thesis makes claims that are grounded in observable system behavior rather than presumed from language quality scores.

## 2.11 Synthesis of Research Gaps

Drawing together the evidence across the reviewed literature, five significant gaps can be identified that the present thesis directly addresses.

The first gap concerns integrated evaluation of multilingual reliability and transactional completion. Most studies of multilingual health technology examine either conversational quality in the preferred language or task completion rates, but few examine whether language consistency is maintained through to final transactional outputs. The failure mode where a conversation conducted in Swahili produces an English-language confirmation is documented in technical literature on language model behavior but has not been specifically studied in the healthcare administrative context. This thesis treats multilingual transactional consistency as a primary evaluation metric, addressing this gap directly.

The second gap concerns design guidance for deterministic control within LLM assistants. While the LLM technology literature is extensive and the hybrid architecture recommendation is emerging, practical design patterns for embedding deterministic controls within LLM conversational systems remain underdeveloped in the published literature. Most existing work either describes purely generative architectures or describes rule-based systems; documented hybrid architectures with evaluated reliability outcomes in healthcare contexts are rare. This thesis contributes a concrete hybrid architecture with evaluated performance.

The third gap concerns patient-facing queue analytics in conversational booking tools. The queue management literature and the chatbot literature have largely developed independently. Most chatbot research treats recommendation as an optional feature without examining the specific design choices that make queue recommendations interpretable and actionable for patients. This thesis tests specific approaches to presenting waiting metrics and examines how users respond to different framing of queue information.

The fourth gap concerns governance frameworks for healthcare AI in low-resource Sub-Saharan African settings. The health AI governance literature is predominantly generated in high-income country contexts and does not adequately address the regulatory environment, institutional constraints, and cultural factors specific to Kenyan and similar healthcare systems. This thesis contributes governance guidance contextually grounded in the Kenyan healthcare context.

The fifth gap concerns integrated artifacts that combine multiple dimensions in evaluable systems. While individual components of healthcare chatbots have been studied separately (conversational quality, task completion, ethical design, multilingual support), there are few published artifacts that combine all these dimensions in one evaluable system. This thesis presents an integrated prototype that demonstrates how these dimensions interact and reinforce each other.

## 2.12 Analytical Framework

Based on the reviewed literature, this thesis adopts a five-dimension analytical framework that integrates insights from conversational AI, healthcare operations, digital health equity, trust and adoption, and governance scholarship.

The first dimension is accessibility, which encompasses the system's ability to serve diverse users effectively. Accessibility in the Kenyan context requires support for both English and Swahili at a level of sophistication that handles real linguistic variation, including code-switching and the use of colloquial vocabulary for medical services. Accessibility also requires interaction patterns appropriate for users with varying digital literacy levels, including those who may not be familiar with chatbot conventions.

The second dimension is reliability, which encompasses the system's ability to consistently and correctly execute its core function: booking appointments with accurate information and without errors that harm patients or damage institutional trust. Reliability requires mandatory validation logic, clear error recovery, and deterministic control of transaction-critical workflow steps.

The third dimension is optimization, which encompasses the system's ability to improve patient decisions through queue-aware recommendations. Optimization requires accurate prediction of expected congestion, interpretable presentation of options, and design that enables patients to make informed choices rather than defaulting to first-available or peak-time slots.

The fourth dimension is trust, which encompasses the system's ability to build and maintain patient and institutional confidence through consistent, transparent, and predictable behavior. Trust requires scope clarity that communicates what the system does and does not do, confirmation quality that leaves no ambiguity about what was booked, language consistency that does not create uncertainty at critical transaction points, and recoverable error paths that demonstrate system competence even when things go wrong.

The fifth dimension is governance, which encompasses the system's ability to support institutional oversight and policy compliance. Governance requires audit logging that captures all significant system actions, data minimization that limits collection to necessary information, escalation pathways that route edge cases to human staff, and documentation that demonstrates regulatory alignment.

These five dimensions are not independent. Strong governance enables reliable operation by providing the oversight necessary to detect and correct reliability failures. Reliability supports trust by delivering consistently on promises. Trust drives adoption, expanding the population that benefits from the system. Accessibility determines whose trust can be gained and who benefits from reliable service. Optimization, built on accessible and reliable operation, provides the additional value that justifies institutional investment in the system.

## 2.13 Conclusion

The literature reviewed in this chapter confirms that chatbot technology has substantial potential to improve healthcare administrative service delivery, but that realizing this potential requires careful attention to a specific set of design, evaluation, and governance imperatives. Conversational fluency, while necessary, is not sufficient for healthcare chatbot success; systems must also provide reliable transaction execution, maintain language consistency through high-stakes outputs, integrate operational intelligence that helps patients make better decisions, and operate within governance frameworks that ensure accountability and protect patient privacy.

The thesis fills identifiable gaps in the literature by presenting an integrated design science artifact that combines hybrid conversational-deterministic architecture, multilingual transactional consistency, queue-aware slot recommendation, and governance-oriented design in a single evaluable system. The five-dimension analytical framework of accessibility, reliability, optimization, trust, and governance provides a coherent lens for artifact design and evaluation that is grounded in the reviewed literature and responsive to the specific challenges of healthcare administrative AI in the Kenyan context.

The next chapter describes the research methodology through which the artifact was designed, developed, and evaluated, demonstrating how design science research principles were applied to produce both a working system and transferable design knowledge.
